# Sensitivity analyses: alternative standard population (1990) and alternative age definition (15-39)

Generated by `scripts/sensitivity_asr.py` from the age-specific panels in `data/panels/` (GBD 2023, cause 1059, 204 countries, 1990-2023). All ASRs are per 100,000 population aged 15-49 (analysis A) or 15-39 (analysis B) and are **point estimates**: GBD publishes marginal 95% UIs per age group but not the age-specific covariance required to re-weight them, so no uncertainty interval is propagated to the re-weighted ASR. The only intervals reported are analytic 95% CIs for the EAPC (log-linear OLS) and for the joinpoint APCs, computed with the same `trends.py` functions as the main pipeline. The 2023-standard series reproduced here matches `results/global_asr_series.csv` to 4.5e-13 and `results/country_asr_series.csv` to 9.1e-13.

| Deaths | 1990 | 8,373 | 352,283 | 2.38% |

**Caveat.** The risk panel J has no 15-19 age group, so this PAF is for **20-39, not 15-39**. It is the ratio of summed attributable numbers to summed total numbers over ages 20-24 to 35-39 (a population-weighted average of the age-specific PAFs), not a re-standardised PAF. For a 15-39 approximation the 20-24 PAF is applied to the 15-19 total: Deaths PAF 15-39 approx 2.26% in 1990 (20-24 PAF 1.42% applied to 47,699 15-19 cases); Deaths PAF 15-39 approx 3.07% in 2023 (20-24 PAF 2.16% applied to 40,137 15-19 cases); DALYs PAF 15-39 approx 2.20% in 1990 (20-24 PAF 1.42% applied to 3,494,234 15-19 cases); DALYs PAF 15-39 approx 3.02% in 2023 (20-24 PAF 2.15% applied to 2,957,873 15-19 cases). That approximation is assumption-based (metabolic PAF rises steeply with age, so 15-19 is lower than 20-24 and this overstates 15-19 attribution), so it should be used only as an upper bound; the 20-39 figure is the defensible number.

Compatibility check: J's 15-49 PAF in 2023 is deaths 0.039561 (GHI panel 0.039561; difference 0.0e+00), DALYs 0.037884 (GHI panel 0.037884; difference 0.0e+00), so the attributable numerators are on the same scale as the published attribution.

## Uncertainty and limitations

- All ASRs in these files are point estimates. GBD supplies marginal UIs per age group; re-weighting them would require the age-specific covariance (and would ignore correlation between numerators), so no interval is reported for levels. Level differences of 1-15% between standards should not be over-interpreted.
- EAPC CIs are analytic and describe the fit of a log-linear model to the point estimates; they are not GBD uncertainty intervals.
- Regional and SDI-quintile values are approximate aggregations (population-weighted means of country ASR) and inherit the instability of small-state estimates.
- Country rankings compare 204 estimates of very different precision; small territories (Monaco, Andorra) remain at the top under both standards.
- The joinpoint model selected the maximum number of joinpoints, so exact years are unstable; only the direction of the last segment should be interpreted.

## Interpretation: are the manuscript conclusions robust?

**Yes, to both perturbations of the standardisation choices, with one exception.** Under the 1990 standard the global ASR levels fall (2023 incidence 70.27 vs 80.87 per 100,000) because the 1990 population is younger, but every trend statement survives: incidence declines slowly over 1990-2023 (-0.27% per year), mortality falls faster (-1.12%), and both accelerate after 2013 (+1.36% and +0.74% per year), with a 2019-2023 mortality rebound of +8.9%. Country rankings are effectively invariant (Spearman 0.998 for incidence, top-20 overlap 20/20, no country moving more than one quartile) and the regional top-five set is unchanged (region-level Spearman 0.996), so the post-standardisation reversal of the geographic pattern is not an artefact of the 2023 weights. Restricting to 15-39 preserves the incidence acceleration (+1.79% in 2013-2023), the female-male divergence (female +2.35% vs male +0.81%) and the metabolic PAF (3.16% at 20-39, which excludes ages 15-19, vs 3.96% at 15-49), and strengthens the mortality reversal (+9.9%). The one fragile claim is the long-run incidence decline, which is significant at 15-49 but not at 15-39 (p=0.22); the text should attribute that decline to ages 40-49.
